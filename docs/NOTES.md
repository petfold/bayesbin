   model. Its state is bounded by merging old run lengths (longer than

   one moment-matched component: in a long quiet stretch every run length stays
   plausible (a Bayes factor near 1 per position), and neighbouring ones differ
   by ~1/√ℓ posterior sd. At 32 per octave, against every run: 30,000 quiet
   intervals 313 components not 30,000 (17x faster); rate, sd, PIT within
   2e-6, 3e-5, 1e-6; with frequent changes in counts of ~500, 3e-6, 3e-4, 1e-5
   (16 per octave was 100x worse there). A trap on the way: bucketing a merged
   component by its *shortest* run keeps it in the first bucket, absorbing every
   run after it (errors of 60% in the sd); by its longest run it ages. The
   NumPy path was then made 3-4x faster per update (2026-10-02; ~250
   components: 170 µs, was 540) by spending fewer NumPy calls, which cost 2-5 µs
   each on this laptop whatever their size: the state kept in decreasing run
   length (a bucket's runs are neighbours, so merging needs no sort, and a
   bucket of two is moment-matched in Python floats), one-count paths for the
   log-sum-exp, the pmf and the checks, and the components kept between pit()
   and update(). pit() is now the larger part: Poisson's betainc costs ~0.5 µs
   a component; for Beta posteriors the cdf comes from the ratios of successive
   pmf terms instead (a cdf by ratios cost as much as betainc for Poisson).
   The remaining error is gammaln(x + A) - gammaln(A), ~1e-10 at A ~ 1e4 (a
   rising product would be exact to 1e-15, but costs more NumPy calls). Left:
   a hazard learnt from the data (a Beta prior on it), fixed-lag smoothing,
   fused (numba) kernels for the updates and the cdf.

## Performance lessons (bayesbin)

- Subnormals were the largest hidden cost. The scaled matrix products (forward
  steps, bin posterior) had a few percent subnormal factors, and products of
  two small normal factors underflow too; with no flush-to-zero in OpenBLAS the
  products ran at a third to a quarter of their speed. Flushing factors below
  √TINY (forward) or below 2^-1011 after an exact 2^500 scaling (bin posterior,
  whose error bound needed the tighter cut) removes them; the bounds count the
  flushed terms as lost. That alone took the NumPy path from 30 s to 19 s at
  T=12096 (one core, an Ivy Bridge laptop).
- In numba, a loop that branches on the model per element (one kernel for
  Bernoulli and Poisson) ran 8× slower than the same loop for one model. Each
  kernel now dispatches once to an inlined body with the model code as a
  constant.
- Without SVML (numba) or AVX-512 (NumPy), exp is scalar, about 12 ns on this
  laptop: it and the lgamma table lookups set the floor of the element-wise work.
- Threads: numba's kernels and OpenBLAS's threaded products, alternating, got
  in each other's way: after each product OpenBLAS's workers spin for a while
  (`OPENBLAS_THREAD_TIMEOUT`, read only when the library loads), on the cores
  numba's threads need, so 4 threads gave 1.5×. Holding BLAS to one thread
  (threadpoolctl) and splitting each product into fixed parts on numba's own
  threads, one BLAS call per part, gave 2.9×. Parts that are too small cost
  more in OpenBLAS's per-call packing than they gain.
- Fixed parts (not one per thread), combined in a fixed order, keep the results
  bit-identical on any number of threads; a test checks it.
- Data with strong steps: the forward pass scales each column's sum by the
  largest row factor times the largest gain, which fails where the two sit on
  different rows. Relative to the one-bin evidence the row factors grow
  steeply (one bin over a step fits badly) while the gains peak at the steps,
  so on counts stepping 2 → 9 → 4 the scale overshot by a median 530 nats and
  64% of columns took the exact fallback (0.86 s for T=1200, any number of
  threads). Relative to every interval as its own bin both are nearly level:
  28% fall back. The fallback is now one pass per column (terms more than 60
  nats below the running maximum not exponentiated) and runs a step's columns
  in parallel: 0.09 s on 4 threads. With stronger steps (rates 20 → 400) most
  columns still fall back, and fast: T=4800 with 16 steps, 31 s → 3.2 s.
  Counts in the hundreds or thousands per interval are such data (a week of
  5-minute slots at 1000 per slot: 79% of columns fall back). Each row's term
  is bounded by its step value plus the column's largest gain, so rows already
  more than 60 nats below the running maximum skip the bin-evidence lookup:
  25-35% faster (that week: 1.43 s → 0.97 s on 4 threads). The bound is loose,
  and the limit is structural: even knowing the final maximum it would leave
  77% of rows to evaluate, while only 2% of terms matter. Picking those out
  safely needs pruning in the style of PELT (Killick et al. 2012), whose
  guarantee is for the penalised best segmentation, not for exact sums over
  segmentations with a given number of bins. Computing gammaln by Stirling's
  series instead of the table lookups was slower (18 ns against 9 ns a term).
- Ported to binsdfc-fb (the reference, the fallback, the coverage): strong
  spike data 0.80 s → 0.14 s on 4 threads. A trap in the port: its bin
  evidences leave out the prior's normaliser (a constant per bin), harmless in
  the gains, where it cancels, but summed over single intervals it put a linear
  trend of 3.5 nats per index into the reference and made the benchmark 11×
  slower until the reference counted it once per index.
- The sd, √(E[f²] − E[f]²), cancels where a rate is large and tightly known:
  at rate 400 ± 1.6 an error in the moments grows 6·10⁴ times. The error that
  mattered was one factor common to both moments: the computed coverage (the
  posterior of the bins covering an interval, 1 in exact arithmetic) was off
  by 2.5·10⁻¹¹ even from the exact path, as the evidences span 10⁵ nats. So
  the sd was 1e-7 to 1e-6 off in every path, the exact one included. Dividing
  both moments by the computed coverage (one more difference array) brought
  the rate from 2.5·10⁻¹¹ to 7.5·10⁻¹⁴ and the sd to 10⁻¹⁰, against a
  long-double reference; the stable two-pass variance would reach 10⁻¹¹ but
  needs a second pass over all bins.

## Where the method stops being 1-D

The exact sum needs three things, and an ordered axis gives all three:

- polynomially many candidate bins (the T(T+1)/2 intervals);
- each bin's evidence in O(1) (prefix sums);
- partitions that break into sub-problems meeting the rest only at their own
  boundary: "intervals 0..k, in any number of bins" meets the rest of the
  partition only at k, and the forward and backward messages are the sums on the
  two sides of it.

The order matters only because it gives the third. On an m × n grid of cells
(N = m·n; a count and an exposure per cell, or s and g) the second still holds
for rectangles: a rectangle's totals are four lookups in a summed-area table, and
its evidence is the 1-D formula applied to them. The data-only factor (Π e^y / y!
for Poisson) is one constant for the whole grid, the same for every partition, as
in 1-D. So what decides each family of 2-D partitions is the third condition.
Families built by nesting chains and trees keep it: their sub-problems are
intervals, rectangles (four numbers, about N²/4 of them) or the nodes of a fixed
hierarchy. Families defined by one choice shared across the grid, or by free
adjacency, lose it.

```
  product grid       rows, then         recursive cuts     any tiling:
                     columns per band                      the pinwheel
  +---+---+---+      +-------+---+      +---+-------+      +-------+---+
  |   |   |   |      |       |   |      |   |       |      |       |   |
  +---+---+---+      +---+---+---+      |   +---+---+      +---+---+   |
  |   |   |   |      |   |       |      |   |   |   |      |   |   |   |
  |   |   |   |      |   |       |      +---+   |   |      |   +---+---+
  |   |   |   |      |   |       |      |   |   |   |      |   |       |
  +---+---+---+      +---+-------+      +---+---+---+      +---+-------+
```

| family | exact sum | cost (N = m·n cells) | each partition once | P(M \| D) | rate ± sd per cell |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| product grid (shared cuts) | no; yes if one axis is short | Gibbs: two 1-D programmes a sweep | yes | estimated | Monte Carlo |
| rows, then columns per band | yes | O(M·N²) time, O(m² + M·n) memory | yes | per level | exact |
| recursive cuts (guillotine) | yes | O(N²·(m+n)) time, O(N²) memory | no: a prior over cut trees | a per-split prior instead | exact (inside–outside) |
| any rectangular tiling | no: #P-hard in general | MCMC | — | estimated | Monte Carlo |
| polygons, regions on a map | no | MCMC | — | estimated | Monte Carlo |
| cells along a Hilbert curve | yes | O(M·N²): bayesbin as it is | yes | yes | exact |
| a fixed hierarchy (quadtree, H3) | yes | O(nodes) | yes | a per-split prior instead | exact |

On a 4×4 grid there are 70,878 rectangular tilings: 68,480 are guillotine, 5,832
are rows-then-columns and 64 are product grids.

The measurements below are from prototypes on the same laptop as the README's
timings (i7-3612QM, 4 cores, numba): Poisson counts on a square grid, rate 1
with a disc of rate 4 (radius 0.18 of the side) and a corner rectangle of rate
0.2, prior Gamma(1, 1).

### Product grids: rows, then the same columns everywhere

Row cuts R and column cuts C, each chosen once for the whole grid. The bins are
the (|R| + 1)(|C| + 1) rectangles, each with its own rate:

```
Z = Σ_R Σ_C P(R) P(C) Π_{bands r} Π_{column bins c} L(r × c)
```

Given C, a band's factor Π_c L(r × c) depends only on the band's two boundaries,
so the 1-D programme over the rows is exact; likewise the columns given R. But C
is shared by every band. Summed over C, the bands are coupled through the whole
column partition, and a programme down the rows would have to carry it as its
state: 2^(n−1) values, or Σ_{k≤K} (n−1 choose k) with at most K column cuts. No
polynomial method is known. Even the single best p × q grid is NP-complete to
find for a sum of squared deviations (Muthukrishnan & Suel 2005), an additive
objective like a sum of log evidences; for the largest block sum, see Grigni &
Manne 1996.

That is the paradox of this family: product grids are a small subset of the next
family (64 of its 5,832 partitions on a 4×4 grid), yet harder. Restricting by a
choice shared across the grid couples pieces that were independent.

What survives:

- **Exact conditionals.** Forward filtering and backward sampling draw (M_R, R)
  given C exactly, then (M_C, C) given R: a blocked Gibbs sampler whose every step
  is a whole 1-D posterior. A sweep costs the two band tables, O(m²·|C| + n²·|R|),
  and the two programmes, O(M·(m² + n²)). Averaging the exact conditional rate and
  second moment over the draws (Rao-Blackwellisation) gives the rate ± sd. P(D)
  follows from the exact P(D | C) by Chib's method or by importance sampling over
  C. Lost: exactness, P(M | D) except as an estimate, determinism. Mixing is slow
  for a weak local feature that shows in neither margin: it needs a row cut and a
  column cut together, and neither pays alone.
- **Enumerating a short axis.** With n small, or few column cuts allowed, sum over
  every C exactly, one 1-D programme over the rows for each: 2^6 = 64 for the 7
  days of a week; 19,650 with at most 3 cuts on an axis of 50 cells.

As a model it suits tables whose changes are global (dose × time, age × calendar
year), not maps: one hotspot needs two row and two column cuts across the whole
grid, and 9 rates, 8 of them background.

"Rows first" as a two-stage fit (bands from the row sums, then the columns given
those bands) is only an approximation: a feature in a few columns is diluted in
the row sums, and the error bars leave out the uncertainty in the bands. The next
family is exact at a similar cost.

### Rows, then each band its own columns

Row cuts R with bayesbin's prior (M_R uniform on 0..M_R,max, positions uniform
given M_R); then each band b, independently, its own column cuts C_b with the
same prior. The bins are the rectangles band × column interval:

```
Z = Σ_R P(R) Π_{bands b} Z_b,     Z_b = Σ_{C_b} P(C_b) Π_{c} L(b × c)
```

Z_b is the 1-D marginal likelihood of the band's column series, its counts and
exposures summed over the band's rows: the band's rectangles are that series' 1-D
bins. So the whole computation is bayesbin twice:

1. for each of the m(m+1)/2 candidate bands, the forward programme on the band's
   column series (evidence only) gives log Z_b;
2. those form a T × T table of bin evidences over the rows (T = m), and bayesbin's
   own `_forward`, `_backward` and `bin_posterior` accept such a table. They give
   the evidence for every M_R, and W[a, b] = P(rows a..b form a band | D);
3. a second pass refits, with rates, only the bands with W > 10⁻¹². The rate and
   the second moment at cell (i, j) are Σ_{a ≤ i ≤ b} W[a, b] times band [a, b]'s
   values at column j, divided by the coverage Σ_{a ≤ i ≤ b} W[a, b] (1 in exact
   arithmetic), as `fit` divides.

Everything the 1-D method has survives:

- each partition arises once: the bands are the distinct top and bottom edges of
  its rectangles, and a band's cuts are its rectangles' sides. So the prior is
  bayesbin's at both levels. There are 2^(n−1)·(1 + 2^(n−1))^(m−1) such
  partitions;
- P(M_R | D) is exact, and so is P(M_b | band, D) for each band. The posterior of
  the total number of rectangles needs each band's evidence per M_b and a
  convolution in the outer programme (about K² more work, for counts up to K);
- rate ± sd at every cell; the posterior of a row cut after row i (from W), and of
  a column cut between cells (i, j) and (i, j+1) (W times the bands' boundary
  posteriors);
- cost O(M·m²·n²) = O(M·N²): quadratic in the number of cells, as the 1-D method
  is in T. Memory O(m² + M·n).

Checked against enumeration of every partition: 5,832 on a 4×4 grid with M ≤ 3
at both levels, 4,367 on 4×5 with M ≤ 2, 2,500 on 5×4 with M_R ≤ 4 and M_C ≤ 1.
log P(D) agreed to 12 decimals, and every cell's rate to 4·10⁻¹⁴. Timings, M ≤ 10
at both levels:

| grid | bands | evidences | refitted for rates | rates | most probable M_R |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 32×32 | 528 | 0.40 s | 262 | 0.40 s | 3 |
| 64×64 | 2,080 | 1.8 s | 719 | 1.3 s | 5 |
| 100×100 | 5,050 | 5.1 s | 1,327 | 2.8 s | 7 |
| 128×128 | 8,256 | 9.9 s | 1,152 | 2.8 s | 9 |
| 200×200 | 20,100 | 43 s | 639 | 2.3 s | 10 (the cap) |

- About 0.6 ms of every band is the call itself (measured on a 4-interval
  series): three quarters of the time at n = 64. A fused kernel over all the bands
  would remove it.
- The most probable M_R grows with the resolution because the disc's edge is
  curved. A rectangle partition draws a curve as a staircase, so M must grow with
  the resolution, and the cost with it, linearly. The posterior average smooths
  the staircase, as the 1-D average smooths a step.
- A trap: `PoissonModel.log_data_constant()` of a band's series (Σ Y log E −
  log Y! of the summed counts) differs from band to band. `fit`'s log evidence
  includes it, so a band's evidence taken from `fit` must have it taken out again,
  or the outer programme sees a spurious term per band. The grid's own constant is
  added once, at the end. The rates are unaffected: within a band it cancels.

What is lost is symmetry. Rows-first and columns-first are different priors, and
the first draws every row cut across the whole grid. Their average (a mixture,
half each) is still exact, at twice the cost, and the posterior weight of each
orientation says which one the data prefer. A partition in both families is
counted in both halves.

Variants:

- The outer axis need not be ordered. The 7 days of a week can be grouped by any
  set partition (Bell(7) = 877), each group with its own time-of-day profile: 127
  fits (one per non-empty group of days) and an exact sum over the 877 groupings.
- The nesting can go deeper (rows, then columns per band, then rows per piece,
  …); its limit is the next family. The outer level can also be a tree
  (hierarchies, below).
- Online, with columns as time: each new interval extends every band's series by
  one (an `OnlineBinning` or `ChangePointStream` step per band, m²/2 of them), and
  the outer programme is rerun, O(M·m²). With rows as time, each new row brings m
  new bands.

### Recursive cuts (guillotine partitions)

Each rectangle is one bin with probability ρ, or is cut straight through,
horizontally or vertically (half each where both are possible), at a uniform
position, and each part is binned the same way. The previous family is the
depth-2 case with a fixed order of directions, and product grids are in it too.
The evidence is an inside pass over every sub-rectangle R, in order of size, as
CYK parsing is for a grammar. For an h × w rectangle, cut after its row (or
column) k:

```
Z(R) = ρ·L(R) + (1 − ρ)·[ ½·(1/(h−1))·Σ_k Z(top_k)·Z(bottom_k)
                        + ½·(1/(w−1))·Σ_k Z(left_k)·Z(right_k) ]
```

Z = L for a single cell, and the weight of a direction is 1 instead of ½ when it
is the only one possible. An outside pass of the same cost gives P(R is a bin | D)
for every sub-rectangle, hence the rate ± sd at every cell (a 2-D difference
array, as in 1-D).

There are about N²/4 sub-rectangles and h + w − 2 cuts in each: m²n²(m + n)/12
terms. Memory is one log Z per sub-rectangle (packed triangular indices), N²/4
doubles: 3.2 GB at 200×200, growing as the fourth power of the side, while the
time grows as the fifth. A numba prototype (parallel over the
rectangles of one size; evidence only) agreed with an independent memoised
recursion to 12 decimals (3×3, 4×5 and 6×6):

| grid | evidence | table |
|---|---|---|
| 32×32 | 0.09 s | 2 MB |
| 64×64 | 1.8 s | 35 MB |
| 100×100 | 18 s | 204 MB |
| 200×200 | about 10 min (extrapolated) | 3.2 GB |

The outside pass should about double these.

Lost:

- **Each partition once.** A partition with crossing cuts comes from several cut
  trees. On a 4×4 grid the 68,480 guillotine partitions come from 2,590,351
  binary cut trees, 38 per partition on average. Taking all of a node's parallel
  cuts at once (a set of strips, as bayesbin's 1-D step chooses a set of
  boundaries, each strip then one bin or cut the other way) removes the
  duplicates of associativity, since k strips have Catalan(k − 1) binary trees.
  That leaves 286,985 trees, 4.2 per partition; the 16 single cells still arise
  274 times. The rest come from crossings: a horizontal cut whose parts are all
  cut vertically at the same place is a vertical cut whose parts are all cut
  horizontally. Counting each partition once would need the parts on either side
  of a cut not to share a full line, a constraint across siblings that the
  recursion cannot see. So the prior is over construction trees, as in Bayesian
  CART (Chipman, George & McCulloch 1998; Denison, Mallick & Smith 1998) and the
  Mondrian process (Roy & Teh 2008). It is a proper prior, leaning towards
  grid-like partitions.
- **The uniform prior on M.** Tracking the number of bins needs Z_k(R) for every
  k and a convolution at every cut (K² more), so a per-split probability ρ takes
  its place. ρ may depend on the rectangle: the Mondrian process cuts at a rate
  proportional to the total length of the sides, which keeps its prior
  consistent under restriction to a sub-rectangle.

The multi-way version costs the same with a per-cut probability (a node's strips
are a 1-D programme over the strip boundaries, run once from each top row for
each column range), and M times more with bayesbin's uniform prior on the number
of strips.

Cuts at dyadic positions only (a quadtree, or a kd-tree cut at midpoints) leave
O(N) sub-problems, so O(N) in all. Optional Pólya trees (Wong & Ma 2010) sum over
such partitions for densities; dyadic CART (Donoho 1997) and multiscale Poisson
intensity estimation (Kolaczyk & Nowak 2004; Willett & Nowak 2007) find the best
one. The price is that boundaries can only fall on fixed dyadic lines; averaging
over shifted trees blurs them.

### Any rectangular tiling

Allowing every tiling adds the non-guillotine ones. The smallest is the pinwheel:
four rectangles spiralling round a fifth, with no straight line through the whole
(figure above). On small grids: 3×3, 322 tilings, 320 guillotine (the other two
are the two pinwheels); 3×4, 3,164 and 3,118; 4×4, 70,878 and 68,480.

No recursion builds a pinwheel, and a sweep across the grid would have to carry
its whole frontier: which rectangles are open across the sweep line, with their
column ranges and the rows where they started, exponential in the width. With
arbitrary weights per rectangle (a prior can set them) the sum includes counting
the monomer–dimer covers of a region of the grid: weight 1 for single cells and
for dominoes inside the region, 0 for every other rectangle. That count is
#P-complete (Jerrum 1987). The single best tiling is NP-hard (Muthukrishnan,
Poosala & Suel 1999).

General tilings buy little. A tiling into k rectangles has a guillotine
refinement into at most 2k − 1 (Berman, DasGupta & Muthukrishnan 2002), and in
binning a refinement costs only an Occam factor, since the rates on either side
of an extra cut can be equal. So the guillotine posterior can fit whatever a
general tiling fits, a little less parsimoniously. If general tilings are wanted
anyway, the route is MCMC with the rates integrated out (split, merge and edge
moves).

One family of free rectangles does stay exact: one rectangle against the rest of
the grid (a Bayesian scan statistic, Neill, Moore & Cooper 2006). That is N²/4
partitions, each O(1), since the rest's totals are the grid's minus the
rectangle's. Two or more free rectangles are not exact.

### Polygons and regions on a map

Examples:

- Voronoi cells around a random number of free generator points (Byers & Raftery
  2002, for Poisson intensities, by reversible-jump MCMC, Green 1995);
- connected groups of the regions of a map (Knorr-Held & Raßer 2000, for disease
  maps);
- the pieces left by cutting edges of a random spanning tree of the regions'
  adjacency graph (Teixeira, Assunção & Loschi 2019, with time).

Bayesian Blocks' route to higher dimensions also starts from the Voronoi cells of
the events and merges neighbouring cells (Scargle 2001, "cell coalescence"): a
search for one partition, not a sum.

None is exact. There is no order; the connected sets of cells are exponentially
many (even on a tree, a node with d neighbours lies in at least 2^d of them); and
a bin's evidence is a sum over an arbitrary set of cells, with no prefix sums
(though it is cheap to update when a move changes one cell). What survives is
conjugacy: each bin's rate is integrated out, so the sampler moves only the
partition. Lost: the exact evidence and P(M | D), the posterior of every
candidate bin, determinism and the error bounds; mixing has to be checked.

Polygons buy less than they seem. The posterior mean over many rectangle
partitions already has smooth contours that follow the data, not rectangles, as
the 1-D average smooths a step. What rectangles do badly is a long diagonal or
curved edge, which costs a staircase of bins (the growth of M_R with the
resolution, above).

### Rescuing the order: a space-filling curve

Unrolling the grid row by row and binning in 1-D works only where the order means
something (a week of 5-minute slots, T = 2016). For a map, number the cells along
a Hilbert curve instead and run bayesbin as it is, with T = N. It is exact and
costs O(M·N²) (the README's 6-week case, T = 12,096, is a 110 × 110 grid's
worth), and `OnlineBinning` and `ChangePointStream` apply unchanged.

Each bin is then a run of the curve. A run is connected, since consecutive cells
are edge neighbours, and compact, since a run of L cells lies within a box of area
O(L), but it is ragged. Every quadtree partition is among the segmentations,
because the curve visits each dyadic square in one run; the other segmentations
merge runs that are consecutive on the curve.

What it costs:

- the prior depends on where the curve runs, so it is not invariant to shifts or
  rotations;
- a compact region the curve leaves and re-enters needs several bins;
- neighbours far apart on the curve share a bin only if it covers everything
  between.

Averaging the curve's 8 orientations is a mixture, still exact, at 8 times the
cost.

Runs in Morton (Z) order can be disconnected, and so can runs in H3's index
order. The 7 children of a cell are consecutive in that order (digits 0–6, 0 the
centre), but consecutive outer digits are not always neighbours: 1 and 2, 3 and
4, 5 and 6 are not, and the ring is 1-3-2-6-4-5. So a run can hold two children
opposite each other without the centre between them.

### Hierarchies, and time inside space (Worldwatch)

A fixed hierarchy of regions (a quadtree, H3, administrative areas from country
down to district) gives an exact recursion from the leaves. Each node is either
one bin, or its children are binned the same way:

```
Z(ν) = ρ_ν·L(ν) + (1 − ρ_ν)·Π_{children χ} Z(χ),     Z = L at the finest level
```

This costs O(nodes), and each partition (a pruned subtree) arises once. Working
down from the root, with π the parent of ν:

```
P(ν reached | D)  = P(π reached | D)·(1 − ρ_π)·Π_{children χ of π} Z(χ) / Z(π)
P(ν is a bin | D) = P(ν reached | D)·ρ_ν·L(ν) / Z(ν)
```

The rate at a cell is the average of its ancestors' rates under those
probabilities.

H3 is such a tree: 7 children per cell, 6 for the 12 pentagons of each
resolution.

- A child hexagon is not inside its parent's hexagon: of one cell's children's 42
  vertices, 10 lie outside it.
- But each finest cell has one ancestor at each resolution, so the finest
  descendants of the cells of any one resolution partition the finest cells
  exactly. A node's region is the set of its finest descendants, a ragged
  hexagon.
- Bins can only follow the hierarchy's boundaries, so a hotspot on a parent's
  edge splits both parents. Letting a node group its children relieves that and
  stays exact: each group of two or more is one bin, from its totals, and each
  child left alone is binned recursively. That is a sum over the groupings of 7
  children (Bell(7) = 877, fewer if groups must be connected); the grouping of all
  seven is the node itself as one bin.

Time nests inside space as columns nest in bands above. In place of L(ν), take
Z_time(ν), the 1-D marginal likelihood of the region's summed counts over time: a
bayesbin fit, or a stream's running evidence. Each region is then either one rate
history with its own change points, or its children differ:

```
Z(ν) = ρ_ν·Z_time(ν) + (1 − ρ_ν)·Π_χ Z(χ)
```

This is exact, at one 1-D model per node. In Worldwatch's USGS replay
(doc/research-changepoint-replay.md there: 24,500 events in 3 months) the events
fall in 1,708 of the 41,162 resolution-3 cells, which have 726, 279 and 85
ancestors at resolutions 2, 1 and 0: 2,798 nodes.

It streams:

- a `ChangePointStream` per node, fed the region's count each window, keeps
  `log_marginal`, the node's running log evidence (with BOCPD's constant hazard
  in place of bayesbin's uniform M);
- after each window the recursion is O(nodes);
- a cell's predictive for the next window is the mixture over its ancestors and
  itself of their streams' predictives at the cell's exposure, weighted by
  P(that node is the cell's bin | data so far);
- the PIT under that mixture is the cell's q_value, from data pooled as far as
  the data allow;
- the cost per window is one stream update per node.

Limits:

- the pooling is the same for all time: a burst in one cell separates it from its
  neighbours for the whole history, so its quiet baseline before the burst is no
  longer pooled;
- given the partition the regions are independent, so neighbours do not share
  change points;
- bins follow the hierarchy (above).

Space inside time is exact too: epochs with bayesbin's prior, each with its own
spatial tree, Z_epoch(a, b) being the tree recursion on the counts summed over
a..b. That costs O(T²·nodes), or runs online as BOCPD whose segment evidence is a
tree recursion (keeping sufficient statistics per run length and node). But
every local burst then needs a new global epoch, which re-learns the whole map:
wrong for local events.

Both at once: the sub-problems are blocks (ν, a..b), a region over an interval of
time. A block is one bin, or is cut in time at some k, or is split into ν's
children over a..b:

```
Z(ν, a, b) = ρ·L(ν, a, b) + (1 − ρ)·[ τ·(1/(b − a))·Σ_k Z(ν, a, k)·Z(ν, k+1, b)
                                    + (1 − τ)·Π_χ Z(χ, a, b) ]
```

with one option only at the finest cells or at single intervals. This is exact:
nodes·T(T+1)/2 sub-problems with O(T) work each, so O(nodes·T³) time and
O(nodes·T²) memory. Each place can then pool differently in different epochs,
and each region has its own change points. On the cascade's bin edges (about 8
per octave, so about 120 over the replay's 3 months of 5-minute windows) and the
replay's 2,798 nodes, that is 2·10⁷ sub-problems, about 10⁹ terms and 160 MB:
seconds (an estimate). As with recursive cuts, the prior is over construction
trees, since a block cut in time and then split in space can also be split first.

### Tried on Worldwatch's data (2026-10-01)

The tree over H3 above, spatial only, on two of Worldwatch's streams counted per
resolution-3 cell (41,162 cells): news events from GDELT (Worldwatch's archive,
4.5 days, 127,367 events in 4,008 cells) and earthquakes of M ≥ 1 from the USGS
catalogue (92 days, 24,500 events in 1,708 cells). Exposure is area × days; a
root above the 122 base cells; a child without events is one empty bin. The
pipeline and a LaTeX draft built from it are in
[paper/twod/](../paper/twod/README.md): `make data && make`.

- **The prior by the evidence.** Shape α, prior mean and ρ over 336 settings: α =
  0.1 (news) and 0.03 (earthquakes), a prior mean 10 times the global rate, ρ =
  0.1, all inside the grid. One pass over the tree takes 0.8 ms (5,882 nodes)
  and 0.5 ms (2,799 nodes), the whole search 0.27 s and 0.18 s. The 1-D package
  cannot yet choose its prior this way (binsdfc's `-P` is not ported); over the
  tree it costs nothing.
- **The best partition** (the max-product recursion): 9,668 and 5,993 bins.
  Resolution-0 bins (about 4.3 M km²) over empty ocean, the Sahara and Siberia
  cover 34% and 30% of the Earth; every place with events gets resolution-3
  cells. A split turns all 7 children into bins, so hotspots carry halos of
  small empty bins, and the base cells have no common parent but the world, so
  no bin is bigger than one. Greedy merging of neighbouring bins (lazy greedy:
  a popped pair is re-scored and merged only if it still beats the next stored
  gain, 0.4-1.4 s; rebuilding the merged node's neighbour sets after each merge
  took 90 s) leaves 1,457 and 539 regions, and the empty space becomes one
  region (78% and 95% of the Earth).
- **What the bins follow.** Where the rate changes and there are enough events
  to show it, not where events are numerous as such. Also how events are
  observed: GDELT places country-level stories at the country's centre, so the
  cell at the centre of the US holds 2,643 news events; the USGS records small
  quakes densely only near US networks. The exposure says what is normal (area
  here; population would split where events per person change).
- **Held-out prediction.** Each record split at the middle of its time span;
  every method fitted to the first half with its prior chosen by its own
  training evidence, and scored by log P(test | train), the joint predictive of
  every cell's count in the second half. For the tree averaged over partitions
  that is exactly log Z(train + test) − log Z(train). Nats per held-out event,
  relative to the best:

  | method | news | earthquakes |
  |---|---|---|
  | tree, averaged over its partitions | best | best |
  | tree, best partition only | −0.017 | −0.015 |
  | fixed grid, resolution 3 (no pooling) | −0.024 | −0.066 |
  | greedy merging, no bonus | −0.10 | −0.24 |
  | greedy merging, bonus 2.3 nats per merge (the maps) | −0.11 | −0.26 |
  | fixed grid, resolution 2 | −1.18 | −0.77 |
  | one rate for the whole Earth | −4.27 | −5.08 |

  The tree average beats no pooling by 1,871 and 777 nats in total. Merging
  over-pools: with a cost per merge instead of a bonus it converges back to the
  tree's best partition. So merged maps are for display, predictions come from
  the tree. The news volume rose 63% between the halves, which costs every
  stationary method alike.
- **1-D: a hierarchy adds nothing.** On 2,048 hours of world earthquake counts
  with bayesbin's weak prior per bin: a free binary-cut tree (the same
  segmentations, a prior over cut trees, O(T³)) has evidence +2.4 nats against
  bayesbin's (a tie: bayesbin itself moves 0.7 nats between M ≤ 100 and M ≤
  200), at 22 s per value of ρ against 2 s; a dyadic tree is 45 nats lower,
  in 0.01 s. In 1-D a hierarchy helps only through speed on very long
  series, or where a second axis hides in the data (trial blocks × time, groups
  of days × time of day: the 2-D families above).
- **What carried over from the 1-D optimisations.** Rows then columns per band
  calls the 1-D code for every band and keeps all of it (blocked BLAS-3 forward,
  fused kernels, subnormal flushing, the exact fallback, O(T·M) memory,
  coverage normalisation, which carries to its outer rates too); prefix sums
  become summed-area tables. Missing: batching the bands, since the call is
  about 0.5 ms of each band, 60% of the time at n = 64. The tree over
  H3 needs none of it. Recursive cuts gain nothing: their inside table is
  O(N²) by nature (1-D keeps messages per endpoint), and their sums run in order
  of rectangle size, not as matrix products.
- **Traps on the way.** A pipeline script named `numbers.py` shadows the
  standard-library module numpy imports, and every later script failed at
  `import numpy`; it is `macros.py` now. bayesbin's fused path needs
  threadpoolctl as well as numba: a fresh environment without it silently ran
  the NumPy path, 2-4× slower, which showed only in the timings.

### Next steps, in order of value

1. Rows, then columns per band, needs no new kernel: an outer `fit` on a table of
   log bin evidences (`fit`'s body for an array) and a loop over the bands. A
   fused kernel over the bands would remove the per-band call.
2. A tree recursion over a given hierarchy (parent indices), with a fit or a
   stream per node: the space × time model Worldwatch needs. Then the block
   recursion, if one pooling for all time proves too stiff. Tried spatially
   (above), and with a `ChangePointStream` per node on Worldwatch's USGS replay
   (5-minute windows, the 506 nodes under the 6 base cells of its 12 busiest
   cells; Worldwatch's doc/research-changepoint-replay.md): the busy cells are
   never pooled, so they keep the cell model's predictions and alarms exactly,
   while a fixed coarser cell costs 5.7 nats a day per cell and 2.3 times the
   alarms. Sparse cells put a third of their weight on the parent; the
   sparsest gain, the denser lose, and the net is small. 86 minutes on 4 cores,
   most of it bayesbin's merging of old run lengths (about 1 ms per update
   under load): the stream's update is the thing to speed up. With Layer 0's
   own model on every node instead (BayesianCount, vectorized; Worldwatch's
   `tree_layer0.py`, the whole tree, all 1,708 cells scored), the pooled
   mixture with a 3-day forgetting time gains 12,448 nats over 90 days, brings
   the sparse cells' upper tail from half its nominal mass to nominal, and
   raises the M >= 5 quakes alarmed in their window from 58% to 72%; busy cells
   unchanged. Now in Worldwatch as an opt-in for count stanzas (its ADR 0005,
   on for `usgs_seismic`): through its live scorer a week of the catalogue runs
   at 137 ms a tick against 543, with P(q > 0.999) 0.00125 against 0.00036
   (nominal 0.001), and injected swarms in sparse cells are caught 11 times in
   12 against 9. Next: grouping of siblings for the halos, and pooling that
   differs by epoch.
3. Recursive cuts, only if axis-aligned grids are the data and the asymmetry of
   rows-first hurts.
4. More data for the draft: a longer archive, a finer resolution from the raw
   coordinates (the USGS catalogue has them), population as an exposure.

## Periodic profiles

For a daily or weekly rate profile, fold the series: days (or weeks) as
trials, time of day as the axis, the finest time slots as the candidate
boundaries. `PoissonModel` then gives the profile with its uncertainty,
which can feed a forecast's predictive distribution. Caveats:

- the days are treated as independent repeats of one profile; if the level
  drifts or some days are exceptional, fit the shape on a recent window and
  model the level separately;
- unfolded, a periodic series needs M to grow with its length (the same shape
  re-learnt every period; see the README's larger problems), so folding is
  also far cheaper;
- a bin cannot yet wrap round the end of the period (planned: cyclic
  profiles).

Other directions: change points along long series, spatial rates over a
hierarchical grid (hierarchies, above), mutual information between streams (the
2005 paper).
